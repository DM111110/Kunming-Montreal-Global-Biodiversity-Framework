Overview of the GBF & COP15

Understanding the landmark agreement reached in December 2022 to safeguard life on Earth.

The Historic Path to Montreal

The Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF) was adopted during the 15th Conference of the Parties (COP15) to the UN Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) on December 19, 2022. Chaired by China and hosted in Montreal, Canada, the framework provides a comprehensive blueprint to guide global action through 2030 and beyond.

Recognizing that biodiversity is declining globally at rates unprecedented in human history, the GBF addresses the core drivers of nature loss—including land and sea use change, overexploitation, climate change, pollution, and invasive alien species—while aligning economic frameworks with ecological sustainability.

"The Framework aims to catalyze, enable and galvanize urgent and transformative action by Governments, and subnational and local governments, with the involvement of all sectors of society."

Core Missions & Vision

1
2030 Mission

Take urgent action to halt and reverse biodiversity loss to put nature on a path to recovery for the benefit of people and planet.

2
2050 Vision

A world living in harmony with nature where biodiversity is valued, conserved, restored and wisely used, maintaining ecosystem services.

3
Whole-of-Society Approach

Involving indigenous peoples, local communities, women, youth, business, finance, and civil society in implementation.

The 4 Long-Term Goals (2050)

Four fundamental outcome-oriented goals shaping the 2050 vision for humanity and nature.

A

Integrity of Ecosystems & Species

The integrity, connectivity, and resilience of all ecosystems are maintained, enhanced, or restored, substantially increasing the area of natural ecosystems by 2050. Human-induced extinction of known threatened species is halted.

Focus: Ecosystem Health & Extinction Prevention
B

Nature's Contributions to People

Biodiversity is sustainably used and managed, and nature’s contributions to people—including ecosystem functions and services—are valued, maintained, and enhanced, supporting sustainable development for present and future generations.

Focus: Sustainable Use & Ecosystem Services
C

Monetary & Non-Monetary Benefit Sharing

The monetary and non-monetary benefits from the utilization of genetic resources and digital sequence information on genetic resources, and of traditional knowledge associated with genetic resources, are shared fairly and equitably.

Focus: Fair Benefit-Sharing & DSI
D

Means of Implementation & Financing

Adequate means of implementation, including financial resources, capacity-building, technical and scientific cooperation, and access to technology, are fully mobilized and operationalized for all Parties, especially developing countries.

Focus: Finance, Capacity & Technology

Target 13: Benefit-Sharing from DSI & Genetic Resources

Unlocking fair and equitable sharing of monetary and non-monetary benefits arising from genetic resources and digital sequence information (DSI).

Official Target 13 Text

"Take effective and urgent legal, administrative and policy measures to ensure the fair and equitable sharing of benefits that arise from the utilization of genetic resources and from digital sequence information on genetic resources, as well as traditional knowledge associated with genetic resources, as appropriate, including through facilitating appropriate access to genetic resources and by substantially increasing benefit-sharing, including as appropriate through a multilateral mechanism, consistent with international instruments on access and benefit-sharing."

What is DSI?

Digital Sequence Information (DSI) refers to the digital database records of genetic sequences (DNA, RNA) derived from plants, animals, and microorganisms. Scientists across the globe access these sequences online without needing physical biological samples.

The Equity Challenge

Historically, biodiversity-rich developing nations (primarily in the Global South) bore the conservation costs, while multinational pharmaceutical and biotech corporations utilized open-source DSI to develop profitable products without contributing back.

The Multilateral Mechanism

COP15 agreed to establish a multilateral benefit-sharing mechanism for DSI, including a global fund. Companies commercializing products derived from DSI are encouraged or mandated to contribute a percentage of profits back to biodiversity conservation.

Policy Implications

Tracking Commercial Utilization

Governments are currently drafting domestic traceability regulations, requiring biotech firms, seed companies, and cosmetic manufacturers to transparently declare DSI usage in research and development pipelines.

Target 17: Biosafety and Biotechnology Measures

Establishing robust legal, risk assessment, and management frameworks for biotechnology and living modified organisms (LMOs).

Official Target 17 Text

"Establish, strengthen capacity for, and implement in all countries biosafety measures as set out in Article 8(g) of the Convention on Biological Diversity and measures for the handling of biotechnology and the distribution of its benefits, inter alia through risk assessment and risk management, and by safe uses of biotechnology, if applicable."

Risk Assessment & Management

Rapid advancements in synthetic biology, gene editing (CRISPR), and Living Modified Organisms (LMOs) require stringent scientific evaluation protocols to prevent unintended ecological disruption and gene-drive escape.

Cartagena Protocol Synergy

Target 17 reinforces the Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety, ensuring that member states have the legislative capacity, laboratory infrastructure, and border inspection protocols to monitor transboundary movements of biotechnology products.

Balancing Innovation & Caution

While biotechnology offers transformative solutions for agriculture, disease vector control, and bioremediation, Target 17 ensures that innovation does not outpace regulatory oversight or compromise wild biodiversity.

Implementation Focus

Strengthening Institutional Capacity

Many developing countries require technical and financial assistance to build national biosafety clearing-houses and train regulatory personnel in modern synthetic biology risk evaluation.

Implementation, Monitoring & Financing

How the global community plans to finance, track, and enforce the GBF commitments.

Resource Mobilization

Mobilizing at least $200 billion per year from all sources (public, private, domestic, and international) by 2030. This includes scaling up international biodiversity finance to developing countries to at least $20 billion/year by 2025 and $30 billion/year by 2030.

Reforming Subsidies

Identifying and eliminating, phasing out, or reforming incentives and subsidies harmful to biodiversity by at least $500 billion per year by 2030, while scaling up positive incentives for conservation and sustainable use.

Monitoring & National Reporting

Parties are required to update and submit National Biodiversity Strategies and Action Plans (NBSAPs) aligned with the GBF. Progress is tracked through transparent headline indicators and global reviews at future CBD COPs.
